# Chapter 3: More Words & Phrases to Use Less or Not at All

## Core Idea

The A-Z entries show recurring failure modes in legal prose: unnecessary
legalese, ornate vocabulary, sarcasm, mind-reading, emotional labeling, vague
quantity, and loose use of legal standards.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **A-Z diagnosis**
  - When to use: when a draft contains a suspicious word not in the Top 50.
  - How: identify the word's failure mode, then decide whether to delete,
    simplify, quantify, or prove it.

- **Plain-English substitution**
  - When to use: for terms such as aforementioned, arguendo, obviated, pursuant
    to, prior to, subsequent, utilize, and upon.
  - How: substitute ordinary words: above, assuming, avoided, under, before,
    later, use, and on.

- **Tone laundering test**
  - When to use: for words that sound elevated but carry disdain.
  - How: ask whether the same sentence would be acceptable if rewritten bluntly
    as "the opponent is foolish/dishonest/arrogant." If not, revise.

- **Legal-standard boundary**
  - When to use: for arbitrary, capricious, facial, frivolous, material,
    qualified, unreasonable, undue, vexatious, willful, and similar terms.
  - How: use the term only when invoking or applying the relevant legal
    standard; otherwise explain the concrete defect.

## Key Concepts

- **Throat-clearer**: An introductory phrase that delays the point, such as "it
  is important to note" or "the truth is."
- **Sarcastic compliment**: A phrase that pretends to praise while signaling
  contempt.
- **Mind-reading**: Language that attributes intent, motive, sincerity, or
  hidden purpose without support.
- **Officialese**: Bureaucratic phrasing that makes a simple point sound
  institutional.
- **Dictionary word**: A word likely to make the judge stop reading to confirm
  its meaning.

## Mental Models

- Use **ordinary English unless law requires otherwise**.
- Think of **Latin** as suspect unless it is the recognized legal phrase and no
  plain substitute works.
- Treat **sarcasm** as a cost: it may amuse the writer but usually makes the
  judge manage tone rather than absorb the point.
- Treat **emotion words** as facts only when the emotional state is legally
  relevant and supported.

## Anti-patterns

- **"Pursuant to" when "under" works**.
- **"Prior to" when "before" works**.
- **"Utilize" when "use" works**.
- **"It is important to note" before something that should simply be stated**.
- **"Most likely because" before a guess about motive**.
- **"Trust me" or "believe me" before a proposition that should be supported**.
- **"Ad nauseam" or "ad hominem" when the plain English charge would be clearer
  and less performative**.

## Key Takeaways

1. Most suspect words fail for a small set of reasons; learn the reason, not
   just the list.
2. Fancy diction rarely improves a weak point and can weaken a strong one.
3. Legal terms are useful tools when tied to legal standards.
4. The safer edit is usually simpler, shorter, and more concrete.
5. A judge should never have to admire vocabulary before understanding the
   argument.
