# Chapter 5: Entry Lookup

## Core Idea

The book is organized like a legal-writing usage dictionary. Use lookup when the question is whether a specific word or phrase belongs in formal legal writing.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Lookup workflow**
  - When to use: when reviewing a specific term.
  - How: identify whether the term falls in Top 50, More Words, or Bonus Material; then apply the category rule.

- **Entry-to-action conversion**
  - When to use: after locating a word.
  - How: convert the entry into one of five edits: delete, replace with plain English, quantify, prove with facts, or keep because legally required.

## Key Concepts

- **Top 50**: highest-priority search terms because they are common and often damaging.
- **More Words**: A-Z dictionary entries with word-specific cautions.
- **Bonus Material**: grouped lists that signal broader edit categories.
- **Word-specific exception**: a context in which a suspect word is precise, legally required, or factually apt.

## Mental Models

- Use **Top 50 first** when time is limited.
- Use **More Words** for a specific unfamiliar or suspicious term.
- Use **Bonus Material** when the draft has a general tone problem: too legalistic, too emotional, too fancy, or too mechanical.
- Do not ask "is this word forbidden?" Ask "what work is this word doing that facts, law, or a simpler word cannot do?"

## Anti-patterns

- **Banning without judgment**: some words are right when doctrine or facts require them.
- **Keeping because it sounds legal**: legal audience does not justify legalese.
- **Replacing every word mechanically**: the goal is persuasion, not a thesaurus swap.

## Key Takeaways

1. Locate the word's category before deciding what to do.
2. Keep terms of art when required; simplify everything around them.
3. When the word conveys tone rather than meaning, delete it.
4. When the word conveys a conclusion, replace it with support.
5. When the word conveys quantity or degree, quantify if possible.
