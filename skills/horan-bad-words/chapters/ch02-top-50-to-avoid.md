# Chapter 2: Top 50 to Avoid

## Core Idea

The Top 50 are common legal-writing words that often weaken advocacy by
overstating certainty, insulting the opponent, adding filler, or telling the
judge what to think.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Top 50 triage**
  - When to use: as a fast pass over briefs, motions, demand letters, and
    objections.
  - How: search for each term; keep it only if it is legally required, factually
    precise, or materially changes meaning.

- **Absolutes require proof**
  - When to use: for always, never, absolute, indisputable, undoubted, total,
    complete, and wholly.
  - How: ask whether the record supports no exception. If not, replace with a
    narrower claim or remove the word.

- **Legal insult audit**
  - When to use: for absurd, baseless, blatant, egregious, frivolous,
    fraudulent, gross, groundless, meritless, and obvious.
  - How: replace the label with the missing evidence, legal defect,
    contradiction, or governing standard.

## Key Concepts

- **Intensifier**: A word that adds volume rather than meaning.
- **Absolute**: A claim that admits no exception.
- **Weasel word**: A modifier that avoids precision while appearing careful.
- **Term of art exception**: A word that should stay when a legal standard
  requires it.
- **Rhetorical flourish**: Style that draws attention to the writer rather than
  the argument.

## Mental Models

- Use **search-and-delete** for Top 50 words before finalizing.
- Think of **clear / clearly / obvious / obviously** as signs that the
  explanation may not be doing its job.
- Treat **frivolous** and **fraudulent** as serious legal charges, not synonyms
  for weak or wrong.
- Treat **very, really, simply, wholly, utterly, totally** as removable unless
  they change a concrete measurement or legal distinction.

## Anti-patterns

- **Calling a losing argument absurd**: show why it fails.
- **Calling evidence nonexistent without precision**: say what evidence is
  missing.
- **Using "mere" to wave away a fact**: explain why the fact is insufficient.
- **Using "many" without a number**: quantify or give examples when possible.
- **Using "indeed" as a monocled transition**: delete it or use a direct
  transition.

## Key Takeaways

1. The Top 50 are not banned in every context, but each should trigger
   suspicion.
2. The closer a word comes to accusation, the more support it needs.
3. The broader a word's claim, the more likely it overstates the record.
4. The court usually does not need emphasis; it needs analysis.
5. A good Top 50 pass can shorten and sharpen a draft quickly.
