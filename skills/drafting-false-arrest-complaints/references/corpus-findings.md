## Scope and evidentiary limit

This reference preserves the derived findings of an August 14, 2026 manual
review of **22 cases and 37 complaint versions**. The underlying case files are
not bundled with this skill. Do not claim to have inspected an underlying
complaint or ruling unless it is available and reopened in the current task.

The corrected set excluded Hayes and Thompson because the reviewed materials did
not affirmatively establish discovery concerning the operative complaint. Do not
treat the set as a success-rate study, representative sample, or causal proof
that a pleading feature produced discovery.

Before using a case as a comparator, match the operative complaint to a
claim-specific procedural source. Do not attribute a ruling to a later or
unavailable complaint version. Analyze the result at the claim–defendant level
because different claims and defendants can receive different treatment in one
order.

### Outcome classifications

Record every classification supported by the docket; the categories can overlap:

1. **Direct pleading survival:** a court held the claim plausibly pleaded or
   denied dismissal on the sufficiency of the allegations. A procedural,
   jurisdictional, mootness, or similar denial does not qualify.
2. **Qualified-immunity pleading or discovery ruling:** a court required a
   reply, found a fact-specific pleading issue, or authorized limited discovery
   without deciding ultimate liability.
3. **Express discovery authorization:** a scheduling or other order allowed
   merits discovery.
4. **Actual discovery activity:** the docket or ruling identifies depositions,
   interrogatories, requests, production, motions to compel, or
   discovery-derived material.
5. **Adverse merits control:** a later ruling identifies a dispositive legal or
   evidentiary problem without establishing that the complaint was deficient.
6. **Procedural or merits-only comparator:** summary judgment, trial, or another
   later proceeding without affirmative discovery evidence.

Category 6 does not establish discovery. Rule 56 permits early motions;
discovery may be stayed, limited, unused, or denied under Rule 56(d). Discovery
access does not prove that a complaint was tested or well pleaded. Losing at
summary judgment does not prove a pleading defect.

For a scheduling order followed by a stay, record the order’s operative
discovery terms, effective dates, stay date, permitted scope, and practical
opportunity separately. A scheduling order establishes only what it authorized.
Settlement is inference-neutral unless a source states otherwise; it does not
establish liability, pleading sufficiency, or discovery.

## Repeated substantive patterns

The strongest complaints repeatedly supplied:

- the exact seizure point;
- the suspected offense and its required elements;
- facts known to each officer at that moment;
- affirmative conduct plus material negative facts;
- separation of later resistance or force from earlier probable cause;
- participant-specific acts rather than collective labels;
- a direct element-level application tied to the decisive facts and each
  officer's contemporaneous knowledge; and
- a claim structure that does not make the court assemble the theory.

The reviewed complaints did not consistently use a formula such as "These facts
support the reasonable inference." Treat the fact-to-element bridge as the
recurring function, not that wording. Use a labeled inference when the bridge is
non-obvious; otherwise state the application directly.

### Direct pleading examples

- **Buehler:** concrete distance, compliance, filming, noninterference, and
  absence-of-force facts negated elements of the stated offenses.
- **Ramos:** separated the initial arrest from later alleged resistance and
  identified which officers performed each act.
- **Rusanowsky:** pleaded physical facts negating obstruction and identified
  collective-knowledge facts within defendants’ control; the court authorized
  targeted discovery.
- **Eaton:** separated facial warrant deficiency from false-statement and
  omission theories; the surviving theory traced knowledge and contribution to
  the affidavit.
- **Jacobson-Boettcher:** tied seizure liability to the arresting participant; a
  later report could not cause an earlier warrantless arrest.
- **Heaney:** showed that a short complaint can survive when it states a
  concrete version of the encounter that conflicts materially with the defense
  version.

Use these as structural observations. Verify the governing authority and factual
record before applying any proposition to another case.

## Amendment pattern

All 11 multi-version cases in the corrected set increased factual volume from
the original to the operative complaint. Five—E.R., Flanagan, Lee, Miller, and
Zinter—ultimately reduced or consolidated their count totals.

The reusable amendment pattern is:

> **Increase decisive fact and actor clarity; reduce unsupported or overlapping
> theories.**

Do not infer that more words caused survival. Amendments commonly respond to
motions, rulings, new information, party changes, or procedural developments.

## Study limitations

The corpus has selection, availability, and survivorship bias. The cases faced
different motions, defendants, judicial approaches, records, amendment
opportunities, and procedural postures. Negative controls are insufficient to
establish that any observed feature caused survival. Use the findings to
generate and audit pleading structure, never to predict an outcome.

## Repeated failure modes

- bare “no probable cause” conclusions;
- failure to address reasonably invoked alternative offenses;
- later conduct used to justify an earlier seizure;
- group pleading that obscures personal participation;
- warrant allegations without a statement-to-affiant transmission chain;
- a post-arrest act pleaded as causing the arrest;
- incorporated video or reports that contradict the pleaded characterization;
- conspiracy inferred from presence or employment alone;
- Monell labels, raw statistics, or theory lists without attribution, notice,
  particular injury, and moving-force mechanism;
- treating information-and-belief pleading as a substitute for known supporting
  facts; and
- treating discovery as a way to create an essential allegation that is not yet
  plausible.
