# Patterns

## Delete the Intensifier

**When to use**: The sentence contains very, really, clearly, obviously, wholly,
utterly, totally, simply, certainly, undoubtedly, or similar words. **How**:
Delete the word. If the sentence loses only volume, keep it deleted. If it loses
necessary meaning, replace it with a fact, number, legal standard, or stronger
verb. **Trade-offs**: A few intensifiers are useful in quotations or when
distinguishing degree matters legally.

## Replace the Label with the Defect

**When to use**: The draft calls an argument absurd, baseless, frivolous,
meritless, groundless, false, fraudulent, or obvious. **How**: State what is
missing, contradicted, waived, unsupported, barred, or required by the governing
standard. **Trade-offs**: Some labels are terms of art. Keep them when seeking
sanctions or applying a standard that requires them.

## Convert Legalese to Plain English

**When to use**: The draft says pursuant to, prior to, subsequent to, utilize,
aforementioned, upon, instant case, arguendo, or obviate. **How**: Use under,
before, after/later, use, above, on, this case, assuming, or avoid/make
unnecessary. **Trade-offs**: Preserve exact quoted language, statutory text, and
doctrine names.

## Quantify the Vague Degree

**When to use**: The draft says many, numerous, generally, largely, substantial,
material, limited, broad, widely, or vast majority. **How**: Replace with a
number, proportion, example set, record cite, or defined legal threshold.
**Trade-offs**: If precision is unavailable and the point does not depend on
precision, consider deleting the qualifier.

## Remove Writer Emotion

**When to use**: The draft says alarming, annoying, appalling, frustrating,
outrageous, troubling, vexing, or worrisome. **How**: Describe the event and its
legal consequence. Let the judge decide whether it is alarming or troubling.
**Trade-offs**: Emotion may matter in fact sections when a party's state of mind
is an element or damages issue.

## Avoid Mind-Reading

**When to use**: The draft says deliberately, intentionally, supposedly,
ostensibly, presumably, mysteriously, no wonder, or most likely because.
**How**: State the observable conduct and the permissible inference. Do not
attribute motive unless evidence and legal relevance support it. **Trade-offs**:
Intent terms may be necessary in fraud, sanctions, criminal, tort, or employment
contexts.

## Kill the Throat-Clearer

**When to use**: The draft begins with it is important to note, the truth is,
frankly, honestly, clearly, notably, importantly, or interestingly. **How**:
Delete the setup and start with the point. **Trade-offs**: A transition may
still be needed; prefer structural transitions over attitude announcements.

## Keep Required Terms Plainly

**When to use**: A suspect word appears in a required legal standard, such as
arbitrary and capricious, clear and convincing, material, qualified, undue,
willful, or vexatious. **How**: Keep the required term, define or apply it
precisely, and avoid extra rhetorical modifiers around it. **Trade-offs**: Do
not paraphrase away a legal standard when exact phrasing matters.
