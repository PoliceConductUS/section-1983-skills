# Chapter 1: Editing Model

## Core Idea

Horan's method is a second- or third-draft editing discipline: remove words that
make the advocate sound overheated, imprecise, insulting, or insecure before the
draft reaches the judge.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Second-draft filter**
  - When to use: after the brief, motion, or letter already contains the
    necessary facts and legal argument.
  - How: scan for words that editorialize, intensify, speculate, insult, or
    translate a simple idea into legalese.

- **Delete-first modifier test**
  - When to use: every time an adjective or adverb appears in formal legal
    writing.
  - How: delete the modifier and reread the sentence. If the sentence keeps its
    meaning or gains confidence, leave the word out.

- **Show, do not tell**
  - When to use: when the draft labels an argument or act as absurd, egregious,
    obvious, dishonest, frivolous, or unreasonable.
  - How: replace the label with the facts, evidence, legal standard, or missing
    proof that lets the judge reach that conclusion.

- **Footnote-level necessity**
  - When to use: for adjectives, adverbs, legalese, and rhetorical flourishes.
  - How: apply the same restraint some lawyers use for footnotes: include only
    when there is a strong reason.

## Key Concepts

- **Understatement**: Controlled prose that lets facts and law carry force.
- **Overstatement**: Language that claims more certainty, severity, or urgency
  than the record supports.
- **Legalese**: Lawyer-only phrasing that slows the reader without adding legal
  precision.
- **Telling**: Announcing a conclusion through labels or emphasis instead of
  demonstrating it.
- **Judge-facing prose**: Writing calibrated for a busy court that values
  clarity, restraint, and support.

## Mental Models

- Use **delete-first editing** when a sentence feels forceful only because it is
  decorated.
- Think of adjectives and adverbs as **red flags for overclaiming** until they
  prove otherwise.
- Treat the judge as capable of seeing the point; do not keep telling the judge
  that the point is clear.
- Prefer a shorter sentence that states the argument over a longer sentence that
  advertises the writer's confidence.

## Anti-patterns

- **Stringing intensifiers**: "utterly," "wholly," "clearly," "absolutely," and
  "entirely" often pile up where one direct verb would do.
- **Court-directed emotion**: telling the judge something is shocking,
  outrageous, or alarming instead of showing the facts.
- **Opponent-directed psychology**: guessing at intent, sincerity, motive,
  embarrassment, or shame.
- **Pseudo-precision**: using a fancy word to make an imprecise thought sound
  measured.

## Key Takeaways

1. Editing for persuasion often means cutting, not amplifying.
2. A sentence can become more forceful when it becomes shorter.
3. If a fact is bad enough, the label is unnecessary.
4. If a fact is not bad enough, the label will not save it.
5. Most adjectives, adverbs, and legalese should earn their place or be removed.
