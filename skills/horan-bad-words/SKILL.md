## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### Edit the second or third draft

Use this skill when the draft already says what it needs to say and now needs
judgment. Horan's frame is not "how do I draft a brief from scratch?" but "what
should I remove before a judge reads this?"

### Prefer proof over emphasis

If a sentence relies on adjectives, adverbs, italics, or verbal volume to carry
force, replace the emphasis with facts, law, or a cleaner verb. Strong legal
writing usually becomes more forceful after deleting intensifiers.

### Show, do not tell

Do not tell the judge that conduct is egregious, absurd, outrageous, obvious, or
frivolous unless the legal standard requires that label. Describe the conduct,
evidence, contradiction, or missing support so the judge can reach the
conclusion independently.

### Treat modifiers as self-incrimination

Modifiers often signal weak confidence: "clearly," "obviously," "certainly,"
"undoubtedly," "simply," "wholly," "utterly," and similar words announce that
the writer wants the point to be easier than it may be. Test each modifier by
deleting it. If meaning remains, cut it.

### Use legal terms only when legally required

Keep terms of art when a rule, standard, statute, or doctrine requires them.
Outside that setting, prefer ordinary English. "Pursuant to" usually becomes
"under"; "prior to" becomes "before"; "utilize" becomes "use"; "instant case"
becomes "this case."

### Do not launder insults through vocabulary

Fancy words can make attacks sound more polished, but they are still attacks.
Avoid labels that call the opponent dishonest, foolish, arrogant, unserious,
cowardly, confused, or shameful. If bad faith matters, say so directly and prove
it.

### Avoid mind-reading

Words like "deliberately," "intentionally," "supposedly," "ostensibly,"
"presumably," "mysteriously," and "most likely because" often guess at motive.
Unless state of mind is legally material and supported, describe observable acts
and omissions.

### Avoid false precision and vague quantity

"Many," "generally," "mostly," "substantially," "materially," "limited,"
"nearly," "widely," and "vast majority" often avoid the hard work of measuring.
Use numbers, examples, record citations, or a defined standard when possible.

### Respect the court's role

Do not tell the court what is "clear," "obvious," "important to note,"
"telling," "remarkable," or "noteworthy" as a substitute for analysis. Make the
point and let its importance appear from the structure, evidence, and law.

### Keep civility as persuasion

The skill is not politeness for its own sake. It is advocacy discipline:
overheated language distracts, irritates, or causes the judge to discount the
writer's judgment. Calm, short, specific prose conveys confidence.

## Scope & Limits

This skill covers the book's editing advice. It does not decide case strategy,
rewrite facts without source support, or replace jurisdiction-specific legal
standards. When a disputed word is required by governing law, use the required
term and keep surrounding prose plain.
